That meant taking a problem from discovery through implementation, release, and production support, with as few dependencies on other teams as possible. I wanted a compact engineering group of roughly six to nine people, but we needed expertise across four disciplines. Two engineers in each discipline gave us eight engineers: two for web, two for Android, two for iOS, and two for backend. This gave every discipline a colleague for pairing, code review, and technical discussion. However, two people per discipline provided limited resilience. If one web engineer was unavailable, the remaining web engineer could continue working but no longer had a peer within the team for specialist review or pairing. We then needed help from another team or a staff engineer. The team owned the customer journey, yet its ability to deliver independently still depended on specialist availability. One option was to increase coverage to three engineers per discipline. Across four disciplines, that meant twelve engineers. With an engineering manager, a product manager and a designer, the team would grow to fifteen people. That would give us more cover, but also more work to coordinate within a single team. Clearer domain boundaries had helped us reduce dependencies between teams. I share this experience in Designing Scalable Tech Teams with Domain Driven Design and Team Topology. The unresolved trade-off was inside each team: keeping the group small enough to coordinate easily while providing enough specialist depth to deliver, review, and maintain changes across every platform. How AI Agents Change Engineering Organization Design? AI makes this possible to explore, but agents alone do not resolve the dilemma. Teams still need the expertise to identify an incorrect implementation, challenge a technical decision, and judge whether the code is safe to release. Reducing embedded specialists without providing that judgement elsewhere would leave the team exposed. I believe the opportunity lies in changing how engineering expertise reaches each team, without compromising customer ownership or technical accountability. Small builder teams could own the product and orchestrate delivery, supported by enabling teams that maintain reusable agent capabilities and provide specialist expertise. A shared platform would provide the foundation. My hypothesis is that this model could enable smaller, more autonomous product teams without sacrificing quality or reliability. Whether it delivers better outcomes at a lower total cost is something we need to validate through experimentation. A proposed operating model for AI-native engineering The design has three team types, with clear ownership at each layer: Builder teams own customer value and the product estate. They combine product, design and engineering capabilities to frame problems, orchestrate agents, verify results, release changes and operate their product area. Maintenance and refactoring remain with them. Functional enabling teams own reusable agent capabilities for disciplines such as web, mobile and backend. Specialists and architects maintain the harnesses, orchestration patterns, evaluations and technical controls. They monitor and improve agent behaviour and support consequential technical decisions. The platform team owns the shared foundation used by builders, enabling teams and agents: delivery paths, environments, access controls, observability and knowledge-access infrastructure. It provides common capabilities that enabling teams extend for their disciplines. Builders orchestrate work for a customer outcome; enabling teams maintain the reusable workflows through which agents execute it. The platform supports both. Builder Teams: Owning Customer Outcomes In this operating model, builder teams are aligned to customer journeys, combining product, design and engineering. They own their product area: discovery, implementation, release, production behaviour, maintenance and refactoring. Builders orchestrate agents across that journey. They frame the problem, supply the context, choose the workflow and judge whether the result serves the customer. Accountability remains with the team after release. In my article The Human Edge in AI Native Organizations, I describe three essential capabilities of a builder: seeing the system, building the context and owning the judgement. Developing these skills within builder teams is essential, as traditional engineering roles have not always required this level of orchestration and end-to-end judgement. A builder still needs enough engineering depth to recognise uncertainty and seek specialist help. This operating model does not require every specialist discipline to be permanently embedded in every team. It requires dependable access to the capabilities and judgement the work demands. Functional Enabling Teams: Making Expertise Reusable A smaller group of enabling teams, organised around disciplines such as web, mobile and backend, would own reusable agents, harnesses and orchestration patterns. A harness provides agents with tools, context, best practices and operating rules. These teams are responsible for monitoring, evaluating, tuning and governing agent capabilities, while providing architectural expertise across their disciplines. They maintain and improve harnesses, while builders orchestrate workflows and retain ownership of their products, including maintenance and production operations. The goal is to make specialist expertise available through reliable, self-service capabilities, not create another approval layer. Builders should handle routine changes independently, with specialists supporting complex decisions and exceptions. Otherwise, we risk recreating the dependencies we intended to eliminate. This model also requires continuous technical learning. Builders must develop the judgement to challenge agent-generated solutions, evaluate architectural trade-offs and respond when automation fails. Shared capabilities introduce shared risks. A defective harness could affect multiple product teams. Enabling teams must therefore treat harnesses as production-grade products, with versioning, automated evaluations, controlled rollouts and rollback mechanisms. When failures occur, builders remain accountable for their products, enabling teams for shared agent capabilities, and the platform team for common infrastructure. Platform Teams: Supporting Humans and AI Agents The platform team provides the common foundation: golden paths, CI\/CD, environment provisioning, security and access controls, observability, reliability tooling, and cost visibility. Enabling teams extend that foundation for their disciplines. It also provides access to machine-readable documentation, architecture decisions, interface contracts, and governed knowledge retrieval. Product and technical owners remain responsible for keeping their knowledge accurate. This builds on the platform and context foundations I outlined in Why AI Coding Tools Alone Won’t Create 10x Developer Productivity. Without them, each builder team would spend time reconstructing context, integrating tools, and recreating controls. The platform must be accessible to both people and agents. Clear domain boundaries remain essential: agents cannot remove dependencies created by tightly coupled architecture. Beyond Team Topologies: What Actually Changes? The point is not that Team Topologies becomes obsolete. My proposal builds upon its principles of stream alignment, reduced cognitive load, and enabling capabilities. The evolution is in how specialist knowledge becomes available to teams. The builder team defines the intended customer behaviour and acceptance criteria. It then uses approved web, mobile and backend agent workflows to propose coordinated changes, run contract and regression tests, and flag uncertain decisions. The builders review the resulting code and customer experience, decide whether specialist escalation is necessary, release through the platform’s controls and monitor production outcomes. If the shared mobile workflow repeatedly misses an accessibility rule, the mobile enabling team improves that harness for every builder team; it does not take ownership of the shopping feature. Risks and Governance in AI-Native Organizations This organisational design aims to reduce routine handoffs within small teams while making specialist expertise reusable across customer streams. Its main risk is that functional enabling teams become bottlenecks by reviewing and approving every code change. Governance at the agent layer must make the boundaries clear. Enabling teams should focus on harness maintenance, evaluation, workflow improvement, expert escalation, and incident support. Builders should own release decisions within approved controls, with explicit rules for when specialist or security approval is required. Review agents can check changes against those rules and flag cases that require human judgement. Uncertain results and changes outside approved boundaries should trigger human review. More agents reviewing each other do not, by themselves, establish correctness; product acceptance criteria, reliable tests and appropriate human scrutiny remain essential. As I argued in AI Made Coding Faster. So Why Is Software Delivery Still Slow?, delivery depends on the whole flow. A smaller product team waiting on an overloaded enabling team will simply move the bottleneck elsewhere. How to Experiment with AI-Native Team Structures This is a proposed organisational design informed by my previous experience and lessons learned. I would recommend starting by testing this model on one bounded customer stream, rather than attempting a broader organisational transformation. The experiment should establish a baseline for delivery time, customer outcomes, defects, rework, blocked time, operational workload and total cost. The assessment must include shared specialist effort, agent infrastructure, platform investment and ongoing maintenance. The critical question is whether a smaller builder team can sustain the same customer responsibilities while improving delivery performance without compromising quality or reliability. I would also define clear conditions for revisiting the model. If specialist dependencies increase, production quality deteriorates or the total cost of shared capabilities outweighs the benefits, the design needs to be revisited. I believe this model has the potential to improve organisational efficiency without compromising engineering quality. My Executive AI Engineering Readiness Scorecard offers a starting point for examining the technical and organisational foundations needed for such an experiment. A strong platform foundation is the first step towards making this model viable. The goal is not simply to build smaller engineering teams. It is to design organizations where human expertise and AI capabilities work together to deliver customer value faster, with less coordination overhead, while preserving engineering quality, reliability, and accountability.
