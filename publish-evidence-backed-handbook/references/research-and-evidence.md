# Research and evidence workflow

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in order appropriate to the claim:

1. Standards, specifications, laws, regulator guidance, and official security guidance.
2. Peer-reviewed papers and original research publications.
3. Official product documentation, release notes, engineering reports, and provider status material.
4. Maintainer-authored repository documentation and source code.
5. High-quality secondary analysis when primary evidence is unavailable or interpretation is the subject.

Search snippets, summaries, generated answers, and unsourced reposts are discovery aids, not final support.

## Source ledger

Record each source once with:

- stable source ID;
- title, publisher or authors, publication date, and direct URL;
- access date and version or snapshot when relevant;
- authority type and whether access was complete, partial, or blocked;
- exact handbook claims or sections it supports;
- limitation, conflict, or freshness note;
- disposition: accepted, contextual, rejected, superseded, or blocked.

Map claims to sources explicitly. A long bibliography without claim mappings is not sufficient provenance.

## Live research

For a recurring or update review, define the previous research cutoff and search only the relevant delta before widening scope. Verify publication dates and distinguish when an event happened from when commentary about it was published.

Research does not authorize manuscript changes. First report candidate findings; then accept only changes that are in scope and adequately supported.

## Gated and inaccessible sources

Use authorized access when the user has provided it and the environment permits it. Never bypass access controls.

If only metadata, an abstract, or a snippet is available:

- record the source as partial or blocked;
- state exactly what was unavailable;
- do not use the unavailable body as direct support;
- seek an accessible primary alternative;
- preserve the limitation in the final report.

## Change proposal

Every proposed reader-facing change should contain:

- category: addition, deprecation or removal, or correction or clarification;
- exact target section;
- proposed wording or exact patch;
- why the change is warranted;
- direct supporting source links;
- confidence and unresolved limitations;
- expected versioning impact.

Reject a change when it is merely topical, duplicative, too speculative, outside the handbook's reader job, or unsupported by sources strong enough for the claim.
